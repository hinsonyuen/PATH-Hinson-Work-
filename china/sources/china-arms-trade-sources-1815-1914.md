# Chinese-created sources for arms imports and exports, 1815–1914

Prepared 10 Nov 2026. Revised the same day to **include only records created by Chinese state institutions** (the Qing court and its ministries, provincial officials, arsenals, the Chinese Maritime Customs Service, and the early Republican government). Records from foreign governments, foreign companies, the East India Company and foreign merchant newspapers have been removed.

**About the links:** links marked ✔ were opened and matched to the catalog record on 10 Nov 2026. Items without a link still need a catalog check, and I haven't opened any volume to confirm the arms lines are actually in it.

**A judgment call on the Maritime Customs:** the Customs Service was a Qing government agency (the Inspectorate General reported to the Zongli Yamen and was paid from Chinese revenue), but its senior staff were mostly foreigners, led by Robert Hart. I've kept it as a Chinese record because it was the Chinese state's official trade statistics office. If you would rather count only records written by Chinese officials, drop sections 2 and 4a.

---

## What restricting to Chinese records means for arms data

1. **Before 1859 there is no Chinese trade series at all.** The Qing records for 1815–1858 are tariff rules, revenue totals and memorials, not quantities by commodity. Arms were banned goods, so they appear only as prohibitions, seizures and individual purchases.
2. **The Customs Returns undercount arms.** After 1858, munitions could be imported only by the Chinese government or under special permit. Government purchases often entered duty-free and may be excluded from, or only noted in, the trade tables.
3. **The gap can't be checked against partner-country data.** Partner figures were the usual way to estimate what the Customs missed, so leaving them out makes the Qing procurement records (section 3) essential, not optional. Arms that came in through Hong Kong and Macau without passing a Customs station will be largely invisible.

So the realistic design is **two Chinese series**: (a) recorded trade in arms and ammunition from the Customs Returns, and (b) a list of government purchases built from memorials and ministry archives. Some purchases may appear in both, so keep them in separate columns.

**Gap:** the Native Customs (常關) were not under the Maritime Customs until 1901 and are largely unrecorded. Overland trade (Russia, Burma, Tonkin) has separate, patchy coverage.

| Source | Holder | Digitized access | Coverage | Limitations |

**Context to record alongside the data:** the 1901 Boxer Protocol required China to ban imports of arms, ammunition and arms-making materials for two years, extendable by two-year periods ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Protocol) ✔). The Chinese-side record of the ban should be in the Waiwubu files and the Customs circulars. Recorded imports in the early 1900s are likely to be artificially low.

## Suggested order of work

1. Start with the **Customs Returns** for one benchmark year (for example 1890). Find the arms lines and any notes on government stores, and check the Inspector General's Circulars for how munitions were recorded.
2. For the same year, list government purchases from the **Haifang dang** and Li Hongzhang's memorials.
3. Compare the two to see how much the Customs figures miss, then decide on the workbook layout (recorded trade and government purchases in separate columns).

## Remaining scope questions

1. **Imports only?** China was overwhelmingly an arms importer. Do you want export data too (small, mostly re-exports and arsenal output)?
2. **Same commodity scope as Japan?** Your Japan workbooks cover manufactured goods only. Do warships, arsenal machinery and saltpetre/gunpowder count as "arms" for China?
3. **Does the Maritime Customs count as "created by China"?** I've included it as the Qing state's own statistics office. Say if you'd rather drop it.
4. **How much of 1815–1858 do you need as numbers?** With Chinese records only, it can realistically be only a list of events.
